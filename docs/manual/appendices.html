A Recommended settings by graphics card #

Studio's defaults are set for a card with 16 GB of graphics memory (VRAM). With less, a few settings need turning down; with more, a few can go up for speed. Any NVIDIA RTX card can run Studio (Part 1); what changes with the card is how much fits at once.

Tested onStudio has been tested on a machine with an RTX 5060 Ti with 16 GB of VRAM and 32 GB of system RAM. The 16 GB settings below are what runs there; the others are where to start on other cards.
Starting pointsEvery movie is different, and long or high-resolution clips need more memory than short ones. Use these as a place to start: if a stage runs out of memory, turn the next setting in its list down one step and try again. The VRAM meter at the top right (Part 4) shows how close a job comes to the limit.

How much each stage needs

Scene CutLight. Any RTX card.
DepthCrafterHeavy. Grows with the Resolution Preset and the Window Size.
SplattingModerate. Grows with the Batch size.
Inpainting (V1)Heavy. Grows with the frame size and Frames Chunk.
Inpainting V2Very heavy. Needs 20 GB or more on the card, or lots of system memory instead (below).
MergingLight to moderate -- unless Use GPU for Mask Processing is on with a large Batch Chunk Size: then it can hold most of a 16 GB card for the whole batch. Off, it stays at a couple of GB, and most of a merge is the encode anyway.
Depth Map EditorSAM 3 takes about 4 GB, and stays loaded until you unload it. Erase's ROSE needs more at larger Size and longer Window settings.

16 GB: the defaults

  • DepthCrafter: the 768 presets, CPU Offload Mode model.
  • Splatting: a full-size Batch of 10 is safe; up to about 20 works, and about 30 for the low-res pass.
  • Inpainting: Frames Chunk 25, Tile Number 2 (the defaults).

12 GB and less

Turn these down, in this order, until the stage runs:

  • DepthCrafter
    1. CPU Offload Mode to sequential (slower, much less memory).
    2. Turn on Process as Segments (the low-VRAM mode, Part 2), and lower the Window Size.
    3. Stay on the 768 presets.
  • Splatting: lower the full-size Batch, halving it each time until it runs; a smaller low-res size helps too.
  • Inpainting
    1. CPU Offload to sequential.
    2. Lower Frames Chunk below 25.
    3. Raise Tile Number above 2 (3 fills each frame in nine pieces).
  • Depth Map Editor: unload SAM 3 (Settings → SAM 3) before starting another stage's job, so its 4 GB are free.

24 GB and more

  • DepthCrafter: CPU Offload Mode none (no offloading) is the fastest. Don't go to the bigger presets just because they fit: 768 still gives the best depth for most scenes (Part 2).
  • Splatting: bigger batches, if the job gets faster with them.
  • Inpainting: stay on V1. V2 is experimental on any card, even a 32 GB RTX 5090: use it only for problem scenes V1 can't handle, never for a whole conversion.

Inpainting V2's offload modes

For the problem scenes you try it on (V2 is experimental, Part 2), V2's VRAM / Performance panel decides where its big model lives while it works. Pick by your card, and by your system memory (RAM):

NoneEverything on the card. The fastest, but needs about 28 GB of VRAM.
Transformer onlyThe largest part streams from RAM. For 24 GB cards and up.
FP8 residentA smaller 8-bit copy, all on the card. Fastest when it fits: about 20 GB of VRAM, an RTX 40 or 50 series card, and the separate StereoCrafter2-FP8 download.
Group offloadEverything streams from RAM. Runs on little VRAM, slowly, and needs about 28 GB of RAM.
Group offload (disk)The same, from disk: the least RAM, the slowest. Keep its cache on a fast NVMe drive.
MMGP streaming (int8)Shrinks the model to 8-bit as it loads, then streams it from RAM. About 30% faster than Group offload on about 4 GB of VRAM, so it suits 8 and 12 GB cards too. It holds about 28-31 GB of RAM while it runs (Windows locks at most 40% of your RAM for it; the rest stays ordinary memory), so 32 GB of RAM runs it; loading briefly spills into the page file.

Remember V2's model files take 46.6 GB of disk space, and FP8 resident another 17.4 GB (Part 2).

System memory (RAM)

Offloading moves work from the graphics card to your computer's own memory, so a card with less VRAM leans on RAM more. If the whole computer slows to a crawl during a job, it has run out of RAM: close other programs, or offload less. Inpainting V2's streaming modes want 48 GB of RAM or more.

B File names #

In your Studio folder

StereoCrafter Studio.exeStarts Studio.
StereoCrafter Studio.batStarts Studio too, as a script.
config/Your settings: studio.json for the theme, the window and your projects, and one file per stage (depthcrafter_auto_sizes.json holds DepthCrafter's Auto Size picks). To put a stage back to its defaults, use Reset to Default (Part 5).
weights/The AI models' files. weights/ROSE holds Erase's ROSE and its own environment (Part 3).
studio_last_run.logEverything from the last time Studio ran; the run before it is studio_last_run.prev.log (Appendix C).
last_jobs.jsonWhat each stage made last, for the Import from … buttons.
dependency/grain_by_theme.jsonThe film grain, tuned for each theme.
studio/Studio's own program files. studio/shell.py holds the extra themes (Part 5).
THIRD_PARTY_NOTICES.mdThe licences of the code included with Studio.

In your project

The folders are in Part 1. Each stage names its files after the clip, and adds an ending:

DepthCrafter<clip>_depth.mp4: the depth map. While it works in segments, the pieces are <clip>_depth_1of4.npz and so on.
SplattingIn hires and lowres: <clip>_<width>_splatted4 holds four pictures in one frame (the original, the depth, the holes and the shifted eye); _splatted2 holds two (the holes and the shifted eye), with Dual Output Only. A D after the number means Dual Eye, an F a flipped clip. With DNxHR on, the full-size file is a .mov; with Container set to MKV, the files are .mkv.
InpaintingEnds in _inpainted_sbs (both eyes side by side), _inpainted_right_eye (from a two-picture splat) or _inpainted_dual_sbs (Dual Eye).
MergingEnds in the format you chose: _merged_full_sbs, _merged_half_sbs, _merged_full_sbsx (cross-eye), _merged_right_eye, or _merged_anaglyph.

Beside your clips

.fssidecarA clip's own settings (Part 2, 8f): <clip>_depth.fssidecar beside the depth map, or <clip>.fssidecar beside the source clip.
<clip>_submask.mp4The clip's Detect Text mask, beside the source clip (Part 2, 8e). It's never treated as a clip.
.partialA file still being written. If a job is stopped, it's thrown away, so a half-made file never looks finished.
finished/Clips a stage has already used (Part 1).
edit_history/What an edit replaced: depth maps the Depth Map Editor's render replaced, beside the depth maps, and the original of a clip Erase from Clip changed, with its erase mask, beside the clip (Part 3).

C Troubleshooting and log files #

Each stage has its own "When something isn't right" table in Part 2, and the Depth Map Editor's is at the end of Part 3. This appendix covers Studio as a whole, and where to look when something goes wrong.

Where to look

The messageWhen a batch ends, a message says how many clips were done and how many failed, or why nothing could be made.
The consoleCtrl+`. The whole run, line by line, one tab per stage (Part 1). A failed clip says why here.
Debug LoggingIn each stage's Help group in Settings. The console then shows much more detail. Turn it on, run the problem again, and the extra lines usually show where it went wrong.
The log filestudio_last_run.log in your Studio folder has everything from the last time Studio ran. The run before that is kept as studio_last_run.prev.log.

Common problems

Studio won't startA window says what went wrong and offers to open the log. If it says the graphics card can't be used, update the NVIDIA driver (version 580 or newer, Part 1).
"Studio could not be started"Another Studio is probably still running and holding the log file. If you can't see its window, check Task Manager for StereoCrafter Studio or python and end it, then start Studio again.
The chapter list is emptyThe project folder isn't laid out the way Studio expects: check the stage folders' names and that the chapter folders start with Chapter and a number (Part 1).
A stage says there are no clipsThey may all be in its finished folder already. Restore Finished in the stage's File group brings them back.
A stage says its model files are missingThe weights aren't where the stage looks: check the weights folder (Part 1, and Scene Cut's in Part 2).
"Driver does not support the required nvenc API version"Your copy of ffmpeg is newer than your NVIDIA driver supports for video encoding (the message names the driver it needs). Update the driver, or use an ffmpeg build from before it, and check with ffmpeg -version which one Windows finds first. Choosing the CPU encoder in Encoding Options works meanwhile, more slowly.
Out of memoryAppendix A, and the stage's own table in Part 2.
Everything is slowLook at the VRAM meter: another program may be using the graphics card. A computer that runs out of system memory slows to a crawl too.
Another stage won't startOnly one job runs at a time (Part 4). Wait for it, or stop it.
Videos won't encodeFFmpeg must be installed and on your PATH (Part 1).

Asking for help

Send these, and the problem can usually be found quickly:

  1. The log file, studio_last_run.log (or .prev.log if you've started Studio again since).
  2. What you did, and what you expected to happen.
  3. Your graphics card and how much memory it has.
  4. For one stage, the console lines from that stage's tab, with Debug Logging on. Select the lines and press Ctrl+C, or use the copy button for the whole tab.
Before you sendLogs contain your folders' names, such as your movie's title. Look through the file first if that matters to you.

D Preview views #

Splatting's and Merging's previews can show the same frame in many ways. You pick two of them, Front and Back, and click the picture to flip between them. None of the views change what is saved: they are ways of looking at the frame.

Judging the 3D

Dubois AnaglyphRed/cyan 3D with the least ghosting and the best colour an anaglyph can give. Wear red/cyan glasses. The usual Front, and the best everyday view on an ordinary screen.
Optimized AnaglyphRed/cyan 3D that keeps more of the colour, with a little more ghosting. For glasses or screens where Dubois looks too dark.
Anaglyph 3DThe plain red/cyan mix: the left eye in grey, the right eye in colour. Strong depth, little colour. Good for checking the depth alone.
ColorCode (Amber/Blue) and (Blue/Amber)3D for amber/blue ColorCode glasses. The two put the amber on different eyes; pick the one that matches your glasses.
Side-by-SideBoth eyes next to each other at full size, left then right, so the picture is twice as wide. For viewing on a 3D TV or monitor (see Full Preview), or with the parallel free-viewing method. X swaps the eyes.
Side-by-Side (Cross-eye)The same with the eyes swapped, for the cross-eyed free-viewing method.
WigglegramThe picture flips between the two eyes, so depth shows as motion without any glasses. Wiggle Speed sets how fast it flips. It holds still while a job runs.
Glasses-Free 3DReal 3D on a glasses-free 3D monitor, each eye at full resolution. Read the section below before you rely on it.

Looking at the pieces (Splatting)

Splat ResultThe new right eye on its own, as Splatting makes it. The black gaps are the holes: places the left eye never saw, which Inpainting fills later. The usual Back. Highlight Holes paints them.
Splat Result(Low)The same at the Low Res size, the copy Inpainting works on.
Occlusion Mask and (Low)The holes alone, in white: exactly what Inpainting will be asked to fill.
Original (Left Eye)The source frame, untouched. It is the left eye of the result.
Depth Map and Depth Map (Color)The depth map as Splatting reads it, after its depth settings. The colour version makes small steps and noise easier to see. Both show the depth's lowest and highest values.
Boost ZoneWhere Pop Out Boost acts, and how strongly. Orange is boost in use; cyan shows what would be boosted while its Amount is 0. An all-dark picture means the threshold is above anything in the clip.
Mesh WarpA different way to make the right eye: the picture laid on a 3D mesh shaped by the depth, filmed by two virtual cameras. It stretches instead of leaving holes. For comparison; its settings are in Splatting & Output Settings.
SBS + MeshSide-by-Side above Mesh Warp, to compare the two.

Looking at the pieces (Merging)

Blended ImageThe finished right eye: the splatted eye with the inpainted fills blended into its holes. The usual Back.
Warped (Right BG)The splatted right eye before filling, holes and all.
Inpainted Right EyeInpainting's result on its own.
Processed MaskWhere the fills go, after Merging's mask settings. Highlight Mask in Preview draws it over the picture instead.
Original (Left Eye)The source frame.
Depth MapThe depth, when the splatted clip carries it (the four-panel layout).
Mesh WarpAs in Splatting, for comparison.

Merging also has the anaglyphs, Side-by-Side, Wigglegram and Glasses-Free 3D, built from the original left eye and the blended right eye.

Overlays

Crosshair (with White and Multi) marks the screen centre, on the anaglyph views and Glasses-Free 3D, to help set Convergence: anything that lines up with it sits at screen depth. Highlight Holes (Splatting) and Highlight Mask in Preview (Merging) paint the holes or the mask on the views that show the right eye.

Glasses-Free 3D

On a glasses-free 3D monitor built on Leia's SR platform (Samsung's Odyssey 3D, for one), this view shows the preview in real 3D with no glasses. Only the picture turns 3D; the rest of the window stays as it is. Each eye goes to the monitor at full resolution, not squeezed into half a side-by-side. It is also in Full Preview, and in the Depth Map Editor's 3D view.

It works differently from the other views, so a few things are worth knowing.

  • It needs the monitor, and the window on it. With no compatible monitor connected, or with its SR software not running, the view is greyed out in the list, and its tip says so. With the monitor connected but Studio's window on another screen, it is greyed out too: move the window onto the 3D monitor. Full Preview has to open on that monitor as well.
  • The 3D lens is the whole monitor's. These monitors switch a lens layer on over the entire screen. While the view is showing, everything else on that monitor (Studio's controls, other windows) is seen through the lens and can look jagged. The lens switches off when you choose another view or leave the page.
  • The camera follows your eyes, only while it is live. The monitor's eye-tracking camera keeps the 3D lined up as you move. Studio runs it only while the view is showing and no job is running. Choose another view, or leave the page, and the camera turns off a second later.
  • During a job it holds still. While any stage is running a job, nothing in the preview may redraw, so the 3D freezes on its last frame and the camera turns off. The frozen picture is lined up for where you sat when it froze; move and it can ghost until the job ends. It goes live again after the job. If the picture changes or the window moves during a job, the plain picture shows instead until then.
  • Where you sit matters. Sit roughly centred, at a normal distance. Far to one side, very close, or moving quickly, the eyes can ghost or swap for a moment while the tracking catches up.
  • What full resolution means here. Studio sends each eye at the clip's full size. The monitor itself divides its pixels between your two eyes, so what each eye sees is less sharp than the same monitor in 2D; that is how these monitors work. For the closest look at the pixels, set Preview Scale to 100%. Fit and zoom resize the picture, as they do in 2D.
  • Some things stay 2D. Drop-down lists, tips, dialogs and notifications that sit over the picture show normally: the 3D gets a hole where they are. Turning the card over shows the plain picture for a moment.
  • It shows what will be saved. Borders and Frame Break are in it, as in the output. The X eye swap doesn't apply (it would turn the depth inside out on the monitor). Highlight Holes and Highlight Mask are drawn on the right eye only, so one eye sees them; they shimmer, which is expected.
  • A little slower than an anaglyph. Two full eyes leave the graphics card instead of one mixed picture, so sliders update a little more slowly than in Dubois. After Studio starts it takes a few seconds to find the monitor; until then the view is greyed out.
For checking, not gradingGlasses-Free 3D is the closest thing to watching the result, but judge colour and fine detail in 2D, and check the final files on the screen they are made for.

E Glossary #

BatchA stage working through a folder of clips, one after another. Also: how many frames go to the graphics card at once.
ChapterA part of the movie with a folder of its own in each stage folder (Part 1).
ChunkA run of frames worked on together. Inpainting fills a clip one chunk at a time.
ClipOne shot of the movie, in a file of its own. Scene Cut makes them.
ConvergenceThe depth that sits exactly on the screen. Nearer things pop out of it; farther things sit behind it.
CRFThe quality setting of a video encoder. Lower is better quality and bigger files.
Depth mapA black-and-white video where brightness is distance: white is near, black is far. DepthCrafter makes them.
DisparityHow far the two eyes' pictures are shifted apart: the strength of the 3D.
DNxHRA video format made for editing that keeps the picture visually lossless. Very large files.
Dual EyeBuilding both eyes from your video, the shift split between them, instead of keeping the original as the left eye.
Finished folderWhere a stage moves a clip once it has used it, so a restart carries on with the clips left (Part 1).
HoleA gap in the shifted picture where the other eye sees something the original camera didn't. Inpainting fills the holes.
Inference stepsHow many passes an AI model makes over each piece. More is slower and, up to a point, better.
InpaintingFilling the holes with picture that fits around them.
KeyframeA setting's value at one frame of a clip. After a static keyframe the value holds; into a dynamic one it travels from the keyframe before, along the shape set in the Shaper (a ramp, an ease, an LFO or your own curve).
MaskA black-and-white picture marking an area: white is in, black is out.
OffloadKeeping part of an AI model in the computer's own memory instead of the graphics card's, to fit a smaller card. Slower.
Put backLaying an object Erase took out back into the finished 3D, at a depth of its own, in Merging.
Project folderOne folder per movie or show, with a folder for each stage inside (Part 1).
RainSplatting's Rain section: the rain separated from the scene and put back at depths of its own (Part 2).
ROSEThe AI video model the Depth Map Editor's Erase uses to take an object, and its shadow or reflection, out of a clip.
SBSSide-by-side: the left and right eye's pictures next to each other in one frame. Full SBS keeps each at full width; Half SBS squeezes both into one frame's width.
SeedThe starting point of an AI model's randomness. The same seed and settings give the same result again.
ShaperWhere you set how a dynamic keyframe's values travel into it: Linear, an ease, an LFO or Custom points.
SegmentA piece of a long clip that DepthCrafter works on separately, then joins back (Part 2).
SidecarA small .fssidecar file beside a clip, holding that clip's own settings (Part 2).
SplattingShifting each pixel by its depth to make the other eye's picture.
VRAMThe graphics card's own memory. Most of Studio's limits come from it.
WeightsAn AI model's files: what it has learned. They're downloaded separately and go in the weights folder.
